# Sanyo monitors and Apple

This file separates direct Apple evidence from later Apple-1 collecting culture.

## 1976: no Sanyo prescription in Apple-1 documentation

Apple's Operation Manual describes a generic composite-video interface, names
Motorola and Ball as example monitor makers, and describes TV use through an RF
modulator. Sanyo is not named. [A1]

## 1976: VM-4092 is historically plausible and has strong owner provenance

The VM-4092 was already a real microcomputer monitor in 1976, independently
confirmed by Smithsonian provenance. A June 1976 VM-4092 also survives in an
original-owner Apple-1 provenance chain reported by Corey Cohen. [S3][S5]

This is stronger evidence for Sanyo/Apple-1-period use than a later auction
aesthetic, while still stopping short of a Byte Shop transaction document.

## 1977: VM-4209 exists; Apple-specific use remains the missing link

The earliest located VM-4209 specimen is dated February 1977. [S1]

The V&A's 2020 curatorial interview says the museum collected an Apple II with
“the same model of the Sanyo monitor that Steve Jobs used to take to trade
fairs.” This is a specific institutional secondary statement, but the published
interview does not identify the event, photograph, or archival evidence behind
it. It remains a high-value research lead rather than a primary proof. [A8]

Stanford's Apple Computer, Inc. Records finding aid identifies an Apple booth
photograph from the 1977 West Coast Computer Faire in M1007, Box 10, Folder 12.
Stanford offers remote digital-surrogate requests, but the finding aid itself
cannot establish which monitors appear in the image. [R3][R4]

## 1978: direct Apple property-marked VM-4209

A surviving April 1978 VM-4209 documented by RR Auction has multiple Apple
property/asset markings. This is the strongest currently located physical
link between this exact Sanyo model and Apple during the early company period.
[S8]

## Later Apple-1 visual canon

The Apple-1 Registry explicitly describes the later phenomenon: famous auction
pictures repeatedly used VM-4209/VM-4509 monitors, creating a widespread belief
that the square Sanyo was the uniquely correct Apple-1 monitor. The Registry
rejects that interpretation and notes that many displays were used. [A2]

That later cultural association is historically real. The repository preserves
it as a later layer rather than projecting it back into 1976.

## Current narrow research question

The strongest unresolved question is no longer “Was the VM-4209 the Apple-1
monitor?” It was not.

The useful question is:

> When is the earliest primary evidence that Apple itself used a VM-4209, and
> for what purpose?

The evidence window is presently bounded by:

- February 1977: VM-4209 exists. [S1]
- April 1978: an Apple-property-marked VM-4209 exists. [S8]

The Stanford booth photograph and V&A accession research are the most direct
next steps. See [../research-requests.md](../research-requests.md).
