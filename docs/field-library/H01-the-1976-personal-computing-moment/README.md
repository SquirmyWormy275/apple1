# H01 The 1976 personal-computing moment

**Audience:** LOOK
**Time:** 20 minutes
**Status:** OFF-DEVICE
**Prerequisites:** S01

## You will learn

By the end, you can put five events from the Apple-1's story in order and say
what made 1976 a turning point rather than just another year.

## Why this matters

The Apple-1 is usually described as the beginning of something. That is roughly
right and it is worth being precise about what actually changed, because the
interesting part is smaller and more specific than "personal computers were
invented."

## Explain what happened

**The order is A, B, C, D, E: 1975, April 1976, 1976, 1977, 2001.**

**What actually changed.** Machines existed before the Apple-1. The Altair 8800
was the well-known one, and it was operated with rows of lights and switches. To
enter a program you set switches, one byte at a time.

The Apple-1 was the first computer of its kind to use a keyboard and a display as
its standard input and output. Not the first computer, and not the first machine
somebody had at home. The first of its kind where typing and reading were how you
used it.

That is a smaller claim than "the first personal computer" and it is the one the
sources support.

**Wozniak's own account of how it happened is worth reading in his words.** He
had already built a terminal with a keyboard and a display. Rather than design a
computer from scratch, he combined that terminal with a microprocessor and some
memory. The monitor program came out of the same instinct: his HP calculators ran
a program on power-up that waited for a person to press buttons, and he wrote the
software equivalent for this machine.

The design is a recombination of things he already had, which is how a great deal
of engineering actually works.

**The commercial reality was modest.** About two hundred boards were made. The
Byte Shop bought a hundred. Wozniak recalls selling perhaps a hundred and fifty.
The price of $666.66 bought a bare board and nothing else, so the buyer still
needed a keyboard, a power supply, and a display before it would do anything.

This was not a mass-market product. It was a board for people who already knew
what a board was.

**A community formed almost immediately.** By 1977 there was an owners' club with
about thirty members, founded by Joe Torzewski, exchanging programs and working
out how to expand the memory. Apple sent its Apple-1 customers to the club for
support. Thirty people is a small number, and it was enough to keep the machine's
knowledge alive for decades.

**And then it was over quickly.** The Apple II arrived and attention moved. One
dealer's account describes the Apple-1 market drying up fast once the II was
introduced. The unsold board that sat in his display case became interesting again
only much later; he auctioned it in 2001, twenty-five years after it stopped
being merchandise.

That last card is really about how something becomes historical: not by being
important at the time, but by surviving long enough for people to care.

**A note on dates.** The dates on the cards are the ones this project's sources
give. Card E's is precise because the person involved stated it. Cards C and D are
given only as years by the sources. Nothing here is dated more precisely than the
sources allow, which is a habit worth keeping.

## Try a variation

Card C says about two hundred boards were made. Elsewhere the same book records
Wozniak recalling roughly a hundred and fifty sold, and the Byte Shop buying a
hundred. Explain how all three can be true at once.

## Check your understanding

1. What specifically was the Apple-1 the first of its kind to do?
2. Why is "the first personal computer" a claim worth avoiding?
3. Card D describes about thirty people. Why does a group that small matter?
