# H01 Answer key

## Part A: the ordering

**A, B, C, D, E.**

| Position | Card | Date |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | A, the monitor written | 1975 |
| 2 | B, Apple Computer Company started | April 1976 |
| 3 | C, boards made and sold | 1976 |
| 4 | D, Owners Club founded | 1977 |
| 5 | E, the auction | 2001 |

Cards B and C are both 1976 and the sources do not date C more precisely than the
year, so strictly their order within that year is an inference: the company was
started around the design, and the boards followed. A learner who put C before B
should be shown that the sources do not settle it precisely, which is a better
outcome than being told they were wrong.

## Part B: which are dated precisely

| Card | Date | Precision |
|---|---|---|
| A | 1975 | Year only |
| B | April 1976 | Month and year |
| C | 1976 | Year only |
| D | 1977 | Year only |
| E | 2001 | Year only |

**Why it matters:** a timeline written with false precision invites conclusions
the evidence does not support. If cards B and C both said "March 1976" and "June
1976," their order would look settled. Given only years, it is not, and saying so
is the accurate position.

## Part C: three numbers

| Number | What it counts | Source |
|---:|---|---|
| 200 | Boards **made** | Owad's chapter 1, and the Briel manual, which says 200 were printed and most but not all assembled |
| 150 | Boards Wozniak recalls being **sold** | Wozniak's foreword |
| 100 | Boards the Byte Shop **purchased** | Owad's chapter 1 |

**All three are true together** because they count different things. Two hundred
made, of which the Byte Shop took a hundred, and about a hundred and fifty sold
altogether, leaving some unsold. Card E is about one of the unsold ones.

The mistake to avoid is merging them into a single figure. "About 200 Apple-1s
were sold" is wrong; "about 200 were made" is right.

## Part D: what was and was not first

| # | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Contradicted** | Wozniak's own account describes earlier machines including his own, and the Altair predates it. |
| 2 | **Supported** | This is the claim the sources actually make. |
| 3 | **Not supported** | Nothing in these sources establishes it, either way. |
| 4 | **Supported** | Both sources say so. |
| 5 | **Not supported** | Nothing here addresses it. |
| 6 | **Contradicted** | Wozniak describes it as a shortcut: he combined a terminal he had already built with a microprocessor and memory, rather than designing from scratch. He says so directly. |

Item 6 matters because the myth of the lone inventor is exactly what his own
account undercuts. He describes recombination and cost-saving, not invention from
nothing.

## Part E: what is missing

Any three. Common answers and whether these sources could date them:

| Missing | Can these sources date it? |
|---|---|
| The Altair's release | **No.** Both sources mention the Altair; neither dates it. |
| The Apple II's introduction | **No.** Owad's interviews refer to it arriving and the Apple-1 market drying up, without a date. |
| Bill Gates's Open Letter to Hobbyists | **No.** Mentioned, not dated. |
| The first Homebrew Computer Club meeting | **No.** The club is described; no founding date is given. |
| Apple's cassette software going on sale | **No.** The nine programs are listed with a price, not a date. |

**Almost nothing else in this period can be dated from this project's sources**,
which is a genuinely useful finding: this library can order five events and
should not pretend to more.

## Part F: the survival question

1. **Nobody bought it.** It stayed in a display case for twenty-five years
   precisely because it did not sell.
2. **That objects survive for reasons unrelated to their importance.** The ones
   that get used get worn out, modified, or thrown away. The ones that sit
   unwanted in a case survive intact.
3. **It biases collections toward the unpopular, the unsold, and the
   unmodified.** What survives is not a random sample of what existed, so
   conclusions drawn from surviving examples are conclusions about survivors.

Owad's own chapter records period owners modifying their machines and expanding
the memory, which is exactly the kind of use that reduces the chance a board
survives in original condition.

## README: Check your understanding

1. **It was the first computer of its kind to use a keyboard and a display as
   standard input and output devices**, rather than lights and switches or a
   teletype.
2. **Because it is broader than anything the sources support and depends entirely
   on what "personal computer" means.** The narrower claim is checkable and true;
   the broad one is an argument, not a fact.
3. **Because thirty people kept the knowledge alive.** They exchanged programs,
   built a software library, and worked out memory expansion, and Apple directed
   its own customers to them. The club outlasted the product by decades.
