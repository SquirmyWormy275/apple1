# H02 Answer key

## Part A: three artifacts

| # | Label | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Original** | Made at the time, unmodified, with a provenance record supporting the claim. |
| 2 | **Replica** | Made later, reproducing the design, and sold as what it is. |
| 3 | **Restored**, and still an original | Made at the time. The replacement is part of its history and should be recorded, not hidden. |

Artifact 3 is where people hesitate. A replaced connector does not turn an
original into something else. It gives it a history, and the history belongs in
the record.

## Part B: what would support each label

| Label | Support |
|---|---|
| Original | Provenance records, purchase documentation, board revision and component date codes consistent with the period, and comparison with known examples. Specialist work. |
| Replica | The manufacturer's own documentation. Usually trivial: the maker says so. |
| Reproduction | The maker's description, plus whatever distinguishes it from a replica in the seller's usage. |
| Restored | A record of what was done, when, by whom, and which components are not original. |

**Replica is easiest by a wide margin.** The maker made it and says what it is,
and there is no incentive to misstate. Original is hardest, because the claim is
about events forty-odd years ago and the object cannot testify.

That asymmetry is worth noticing: the honest label is also the cheap one.

## Part C: the harder cases

| # | Label | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | **Replica**, or reproduction, built from period parts | The parts are original; the board is not. Age of components does not make the object old. This is genuinely arguable and the arguing is the value. |
| 5 | **Original, heavily restored** | Still an original by continuity, and the record has to carry the full replacement history. Some collectors would dispute this; that dispute is real and this lesson does not settle it. |
| 6 | **A problem** | Aged to deceive and sold without description. Whatever it is, the presentation is the issue. |
| 7 | **Reproduction**, honestly sold | Same object, described accurately. |

**Items 6 and 7 are the same object and the difference is the description.** That
is the lesson's central point stated as plainly as it can be. Nothing about the
physical thing changed. One is a legitimate reproduction and one is not, and the
words did all the work.

Item 4 deserves discussion rather than a verdict. Reasonable people differ, and a
learner who argues either way with a stated reason has done the exercise
correctly.

## Part D: rewrite the listing

1. **"Apple-1 computer, working, 1976."**
   Missing: whether it is an original or a replica; what "working" means and when
   it was last verified; what evidence supports the 1976 date. Accurate version
   depends on answers that are not given.

2. **"Genuine Apple 1 replica, original condition."**
   "Genuine replica" is not a contradiction, though it reads oddly: it means a
   replica genuinely made by whoever it claims. "Original condition" is the
   problem: original to what? A replica has no original condition in the sense
   collectors mean. Better: "Replica 1 Plus, unmodified since manufacture."

3. **"Rare Apple-1 board, all original parts, fully restored."**
   **The contradiction is "all original parts" and "fully restored."** Restoration
   generally means something was replaced or repaired. If every part is original,
   what was restored? Either some parts are not original, or "restored" means only
   cleaning, and the listing should say which.

## Part E: this project's machine

1. **Replica.**
2. **Its own manual**, the Briel Computers Replica 1 Plus User Manual, June 2014,
   held in this project's collection.
3. One sentence, for example:

   > Replica 1 Plus single-board computer, Briel Computers, manufactured after
   > 2014, retained with its user manual.

4. **Everything in it needs evidence except the words that come from the manual.**
   "Manufactured after 2014" needs a board date or a purchase record; the
   repository's preservation dossier asks for exactly that in its baseline
   inventory: board revision, serial if present, and photographs. "Retained with
   its user manual" needs the manual's identity established, and this library
   already records an open item there: the collection folder's filename differs
   from the one in the project index and no hash comparison has been run.

## Part F: the boundary of this lesson

| # | Can this lesson answer it? |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Yes.** Vocabulary is what it teaches. |
| 2 | **No.** It makes no authentication claim about any object. |
| 3 | **Partly.** Part B names the kinds of evidence a specialist uses. It does not teach how to evaluate them. |
| 4 | **No.** This library makes no claim about value. |
| 5 | **Yes.** No. Most mislabeling is repetition, looseness, or inherited error. |

## Part G: the correction letter

Acceptance: correct, specific, brief, and not accusatory. It should assume the
error was inherited rather than intended.

A sample:

> I noticed the label for the single-board computer in case 4 describes it as an
> Apple-1. From the photographs it appears to be a Briel Replica 1 Plus, which is
> a modern reproduction of that design rather than one of the 1976 boards. If
> that is right, "Replica 1 Plus, after Wozniak's 1976 Apple-1 design" would be
> more accurate, and I would be glad to be corrected if the case holds something
> I have misread.

Note what it does: states the observation, gives the reason, offers a specific
fix, and leaves room to be wrong. The last clause is not politeness padding. The
writer has not examined the object and should say so.

## Try a variation

Acceptance: the learner used "replica" for the machine, did not call it an
Apple-1 unqualified, described any work done as work done rather than as
restoration of an original, and marked the evidence-needing parts. Most learners
under-mark; almost every factual clause needs something.

## README: Check your understanding

1. **That it was made at the time by the people who made them.** It is a claim
   about history, and it needs evidence like any other.
2. **Because the word declares what the object is.** A replica sold as a replica
   deceives nobody, and the maker has no reason to misstate it.
3. **Yes, it is still an original**, and it now has a history that should be
   recorded: which components, when, by whom, and why. Some collectors weigh heavy
   replacement differently, and that disagreement is real rather than a matter of
   getting the definition wrong.
