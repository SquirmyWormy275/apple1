# H02 Original, replica, and reproduction

**Audience:** STUDY
**Time:** 40 minutes
**Status:** OFF-DEVICE
**Prerequisites:** S01, S04

## You will learn

By the end, you can use four words precisely for historically different objects,
and say what each label claims and what would be needed to support it.

## Why this matters

These words get used loosely, and the looseness is where trouble starts. A
replica honestly described is an admirable object. The same object described as
an original is a problem, and the difference is entirely in the words.

Getting this right is a courtesy to collectors, who care about it a great deal,
and it is protection for you, because the person most likely to be misled by a
sloppy label is whoever owns the thing next.

## Explain what happened

**Original** means made at the time by the people who made them. For the Apple-1
that is one of roughly two hundred boards from 1976. Calling something an
original is a claim about its history, and like any claim it needs evidence.

**Replica** means made later to reproduce the design, and sold as what it is. The
honesty is built into the word. A Replica 1 Plus is a replica, its manual says so,
and nobody is deceived.

**Reproduction** usually means a closer copy, reproducing appearance as well as
function. The distinction from replica is soft and people use both words loosely.
What matters is the principle: the closer a copy gets to the original in
appearance, the more work the label has to do, because the object itself has
stopped doing it.

**Restored** means an original that has been repaired or partly replaced. It is
still an original. But a restored object carries a history, and that history
should travel with it. Which parts are original? What was replaced, when, and by
whom? Those questions have answers, and the answers are part of what the object
is.

**This project's machine is a replica.** A Replica 1 Plus, documented by its own
manual as a modern reproduction. Nothing in this library should describe it as an
Apple-1 without qualification, and S01 exists partly to establish that habit.

**On authentication, and why this lesson does not do it.** Deciding whether a
particular board is an original is specialist work. It involves board revisions,
component date codes, solder characteristics, provenance records, and comparison
with known examples, and people who do it professionally are careful and
sometimes still uncertain.

**This lesson makes no authentication claims and teaches no authentication
method.** What it teaches is the vocabulary, and the discipline of asking what is
being claimed and by whom. If you need to know whether an object is original, the
answer is to consult someone who does that work, not to apply a checklist from a
lesson.

**Why collectors care so much.** Partly value, and it would be dishonest to
pretend otherwise. But also because a collection is a record. An object with a
wrong label does not just misprice itself; it corrupts what the collection says
about the past. A replica catalogued as an original is a small false statement
that outlives everyone involved.

**The generous reading is usually the right one.** Most mislabeling is not fraud.
It is somebody repeating what they were told, or using a word loosely, or
inheriting an object with a note that was already wrong. Correcting a label is
usually a favour, not an accusation, and it is worth doing in that spirit.
