# H05 The future history you are making

**Audience:** LOOK
**Time:** 20 minutes
**Status:** OFF-DEVICE
**Prerequisites:** H03

## You will learn

By the end, you can write a collection log entry that separates what you did,
what you saw, and what you think it meant.

## Why this matters

Everything in the H-series has been about records other people left. The
Owners Club newsletter, a dealer's recollection of a board in a display case, a
manual from 2014. Every one of those was somebody's ordinary paperwork at the
time.

Your notes are the same kind of thing. Not because this project is famous, but
because that is how the record of anything gets made: someone wrote it down while
it was still boring.

## First result

One log entry, written today, about something you actually did.

## What you need

Paper. `assets/log-entry.txt`. Nothing powered on.

## Activity

1. Read the six fields on `assets/log-entry.txt`.
2. Fill one in about something you did today with this project, even if it was
   only reading a lesson.
3. Fill in "what I am not sure about." That is your first result.

## Explain what happened

**Three fields that people collapse into one.** What you did, what you saw, and
what you thought it meant are three different things, and a log that runs them
together is much less useful later.

"The display garbled when I opened the port" is what happened. "The serial path
is broken" is what somebody thought it meant. Six months on, the first is still
true and the second may not be, and if they are in the same sentence you cannot
separate them any more.

This is the preservation dossier's own rule: record facts separately from
hypotheses and vendor claims.

**"What I am not sure about" is the most valuable line.** It is the one that
tells a future reader, possibly you, where to be careful. A confident log invites
confident conclusions. A log that says "I could not tell whether the flicker was
the monitor or the board" hands over something genuinely useful.

**Log the day nothing happened.** This sounds like busywork and is not. A gap in
a log is ambiguous: did nothing happen, or did somebody forget to write? An entry
saying "read two lessons, powered nothing on" removes that ambiguity for the cost
of one line.

**Log what you decided not to do.** "Considered checking the ROM contents,
decided not to because there is no approved procedure" is a real record. It tells
a future reader the question was asked, which stops them wondering whether anyone
thought of it.

**Photograph before, not after.** The photograph you want is always the one from
before the change, and you only know that afterwards. The project's preservation
dossier asks for a photograph before and after any intentional change, and the
"before" is the one that gets forgotten.

**The version of every file you used.** This library will change. If you note
"followed A05 as of today," a future reader can find that version. Without it
they read whatever A05 says by then and wonder why your account does not match.

**A careful hobbyist is a good archivist.** That is not a consolation prize. Most
of what survives about early home computing was kept by people who were not
historians and did not think of themselves as doing anything special. The Apple I
Owners Club was thirty people exchanging programs. Their newsletter is a source
now.

You do not need to be doing anything remarkable. You need to write down what you
did, plainly, while you still remember it. That is the whole skill, and it is
available to anyone with a notebook.

## Try a variation

Find the oldest note you have about any project of your own. Read it. Mark every
place you cannot now tell whether something was observed or assumed. That is what
your current notes will look like to you later.

## Check your understanding

1. Why keep "what I saw" separate from "what I thought it meant"?
2. Why log a day when nothing happened?
3. Why note which version of a document you followed?

## Sources and boundaries

The evidence rules and the photograph requirement are this repository's; the
Owners Club material comes from Owad. Citations in `SOURCE-NOTES.md`.

What this lesson does **not** establish:

- Nothing about this project's machine.
- It authorizes no firmware load, EEPROM write, CFFA1 write, serial-port open, or
  physical modification. A log entry records what was done; it does not permit
  anything.
