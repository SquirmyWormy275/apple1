# H06 The monitor that wasn't

**Audience:** STUDY  
**Time:** 35 minutes  
**Status:** OFF-DEVICE  
**Prerequisites:** S04, H02, H03

## You will learn

By the end, you can explain why the Sanyo VM-4209 can be an iconic Apple-1
display without having been Apple's official Apple-1 monitor.

## Why this matters

Historical photographs, museum displays, and auction ensembles can create a
strong visual convention. That convention is useful evidence about later
collecting culture, but it is not automatically evidence about what a buyer
received in 1976.

## Activity

1. Read `ACTIVITY.md` and compare Apple's 1976 display instructions with the
   later VM-4209 evidence.
2. Build a five-event evidence timeline.
3. Explain in one sentence why a 1979 VM-4209 beside a 1976 Apple-1 can be a
   legitimate museum ensemble without being an original 1976 pairing.

## Explain what happened

Apple's 1976 Operation Manual described a general composite-video interface,
not a proprietary monitor. It explicitly discussed standard video monitors,
home televisions through RF modulators, and named Motorola and Ball as examples.
The VM-4209 appears later in the documented timeline: surviving specimens date
from 1977, explicit computer-retail advertising is documented by early 1978,
and an April 1978 example survives with Apple property markings. Later Apple-1
auctions and collections repeatedly used square Sanyo monitors, helping the
VM-4209 become visually canonical.

Those statements do not conflict. They describe different historical layers.
