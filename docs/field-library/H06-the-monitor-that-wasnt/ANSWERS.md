# H06 answers

## Part A

1. “Apple's manual supported a standard composite monitor or a TV through an
   RF modulator.” — **FACT**.
2. “VM-4209/VM-4509 monitors became common in famous Apple-1 auction images.” —
   **LATER ASSOCIATION**.
3. “Apple bundled a VM-4209 with Apple-1 computers in 1976.” — **UNVERIFIED**;
   the project has found no primary transaction evidence for that claim.

## Part B

1. Smithsonian VM-4092 manufactured — **February 1976** — institutional object
   record.
2. Apple explains generic monitor / TV use — **1976** — primary Apple manual.
3. Earliest currently recorded VM-4209 specimen — **February 1977** — specimen
   provenance.
4. VM-4209 explicitly listed in BYTE computer advertising — **January 1978** in
   the audited source register — primary contemporary advertisement.
5. Apple-property-marked VM-4209 manufactured — **April 1978** — artifact
   provenance documented by later auction record.

## Part C

A sound answer is:

1. The ensemble proves that the museum's Apple-1 is now associated/displayed
   with that VM-4209.
2. The date rules out the monitor being an original 1976-manufactured companion.
3. Stronger evidence would be a dated owner photograph, receipt, correspondence,
   inventory, or other provenance record connecting that particular monitor to
   the Apple-1 at that time.

## Part D

Example:

> The Apple-1 did not have one official monitor. Apple's 1976 manual supported
> standard composite monitors and TVs through RF modulators. Sanyo's VM-4209
> appeared in the late-1970s computer market, later gained real Apple provenance,
> and became a popular Apple-1 collector display, but it is not documented as
> Apple's 1976 bundled monitor.

## Check-your-understanding answers

1. **No.**
2. **February 1977**, based on the earliest currently recorded dated specimen.
3. The tag shows an Apple association for that monitor, but not which Apple
   product, department, date of acquisition, or use case.
