# X04 Curate a mini software exhibit

**Audience:** STUDY
**Time:** 60 minutes
**Status:** OFF-DEVICE
**Prerequisites:** S04, H02, H03

## You will learn

By the end, you can choose three artifacts that say something together, describe
and credit each one honestly, and write down what each does not prove.

## Why this matters

An exhibit is an argument made out of objects. Choosing three is a claim that
these three, together, show something. Getting that right means being precise
about what each one is, where it came from, and what it does not establish.

It is also where several strands of this library meet: S04's sorting, H02's
vocabulary, and H03's evidence records, all applied to something a stranger will
read.

## First result

Three artifacts chosen, with a one-sentence reason for each being in the set.

## What you need

Paper. `assets/exhibit-card.txt`, three copies. The artifacts available in this
project, listed in `ACTIVITY.md` Part A.

## Activity

1. Read the nine fields on `assets/exhibit-card.txt`.
2. Choose three artifacts from the list in `ACTIVITY.md` Part A.
3. For each, write one sentence on why it is in the set. That is your first
   result.

## Explain what happened

**Three is a set, not a sample.** Two artifacts invite comparison; four start to
be a survey. Three is enough to make a point and few enough that each has to earn
its place.

The question for each is not "is this interesting" but "what does this show that
the other two do not." An artifact that duplicates another's job should be cut.

**Titles are read; everything else is skimmed.** The title field is the only one
you can rely on a visitor reading, so it has to be accurate on its own. "Apple-1
line input program" is wrong if the artifact is a byte list for a replica.
"Twenty-six bytes that read a line of typing" is right and more interesting.

**Provenance travels with the object.** Fields 4 and 5 are H03's evidence card in
public-facing form. Where it came from, who made it, and who says so. "Found in
the project folder" is an honest answer if it is the true one, and better than a
confident guess about origin.

**Rights are the field people skip, and it matters.** Field 6 asks whether the
artifact may be displayed or copied, and who would know. "I found it online" is a
location, not a permission. A vendor's firmware source is somebody's work. A
scanned manual is somebody's document.

This library cannot answer those questions for you and does not pretend to. What
it asks is that you write down what you know and what you would have to find out,
rather than leaving the field blank and letting a reader assume it was checked.

The repository asks for the same thing: alongside each archive manifest, a note
covering the source, the permission, and any licence.

**The status label belongs on the card.** If an artifact is a RAM-only candidate
with no live-run authority, a visitor reading about it should see that. An exhibit
that presents a program without saying whether anyone may run it invites the
question and answers it wrongly by omission.

**Field 9 is the one that makes this an exhibit rather than a display.** What does
this artifact not prove?

A byte list does not prove the machine runs it. A manual does not prove the board
matches it. A recorded emulator run does not prove anything about hardware. A
photograph does not prove what it appears to show unless you know when and where
it was taken.

Writing that on the card is unusual and it is the honest thing to do. Every
artifact in a collection is silently offered as evidence for something. Naming the
limit is how you stop a visitor drawing a conclusion the object cannot support.

**A caution about your own exhibit.** The temptation is to choose three artifacts
that agree. A better set often includes one that complicates the story: a source
archive that may or may not match the installed firmware, for instance, is more
honest than three items that all point the same way.

## Try a variation

Write field 9 for all three of your artifacts first, before anything else. See
whether that changes which three you would choose.

## Check your understanding

1. Why three rather than five?
2. "I found it online" answers which field, and which field does it not answer?
3. Why does a status label belong on a card a visitor reads?

## Sources and boundaries

The artifacts listed are this project's; the record requirements are its own.
Citations in `SOURCE-NOTES.md`.

What this lesson does **not** establish or authorize:

- **It resolves no rights or permission question.** It asks them; answering them
  requires information this library does not hold.
- It authenticates nothing and makes no claim about value.
- It authorizes no firmware load, EEPROM write, CFFA1 write, serial-port open, or
  physical modification. No artifact is displayed on, or run by, any machine.
